# Collaborative Discussion 3 — Initial Post

**Topic:** Deep Learning  
**Unit:** 9  
**Status:** Submitted

## Activity brief

The discussion examined whether generative technologies based on deep learning, including systems that generate text and images, raise ethical issues that should be considered.

**Initial-post requirement:** At least 200 words, labelled **Initial Post**, with appropriate citations and references.

## Initial Post

Generative technologies based on deep learning create substantial opportunities, but they also raise ethical issues that should be considered throughout their design and deployment. The main concern is not that systems such as ChatGPT or image generators can create new content, but that they can do so at scale while reproducing problems inherited from their training data and deployment context.

One issue is bias. Large generative models learn statistical patterns from extensive datasets, which may include stereotypes, unequal representation and harmful associations. These problems can subsequently be reproduced in generated text or images. Bender et al. (2021) argue that large language models can reproduce biases present in their training data and warn that increasing model scale does not remove these underlying risks. Bommasani et al. (2021) similarly note that foundation models can distribute the same defects across many downstream applications.

Privacy and intellectual property also require attention. Training datasets may contain personal, copyrighted or otherwise sensitive material. Importantly, generative models do not always produce entirely novel outputs. Carlini et al. (2023) demonstrated that diffusion models can memorise and reproduce individual examples from their training data. This creates concerns regarding consent, privacy, ownership and whether creators should have greater control over the use of their work.

Another issue concerns misinformation and authenticity. Generative systems can produce convincing text, images, audio and video at very low cost. Deepfakes may therefore facilitate fraud, impersonation or manipulation and can make it more difficult to distinguish genuine evidence from synthetic media (Chesney and Citron, 2019).

However, these risks do not mean that generative AI should simply be rejected. The same technologies can support education, creativity, accessibility, research and productivity. The ethical challenge is therefore to reduce foreseeable harm while retaining legitimate benefits. Measures could include improved dataset documentation, privacy-preserving training, provenance information for generated media, stronger safeguards against impersonation, human review for high-impact uses and clear accountability for organisations deploying these systems.

Overall, I consider generative AI to be a sociotechnical issue rather than purely a technical one. Ethical evaluation should consider not only what a model can generate, but whose data was used, who may be affected by its outputs, how mistakes can be detected and who is responsible when harm occurs.
